# Cosmetic Surgery Recovery page: direct-response copy review

Reviewed: `pages/01-landing-cosmetic-surgery-recovery.html` after the 13 September 2026 rewrite. Method: the direct-response tests (headline formula, opening line, pain quantification, the So-What chain, rhythm, specificity, open loops, CTA structure, disqualification, AI tells, the read-aloud test) plus Schwartz's awareness levels. This is a review of copy that already passed a CRO restructure, so the bar is "would a stranger read it as a person talking," not "is it on message."

**Overall: 7 / 10.** The page is on message, specific where it counts, and honest. What it lacks is pull. Almost every sentence is a closed statement, so the reader is informed rather than carried. The fixes below are mostly additions of two to three sentences and a handful of word swaps, not a rewrite.

## 1. Headline

> Your surgeon did their part. The next few weeks decide the result.

**What works.** Stakes and contrast in eleven words. It enters the conversation already in their head (I paid for this; is it going to turn out?). It's a story headline in miniature: someone else did their job, now it's your move.

**What's missing against the formula.** No specific timeframe and no self-selection. "The next few weeks" is true but soft; a reader can't picture "few." And nothing in the headline says cosmetic surgery, so the eyebrow and the procedure chips are doing the callout work.

**Recommendation.** Keep the structure, sharpen the number once Dr. Haas confirms the window he'd stand behind:

> Your surgeon did their part. The next three weeks decide the result.

If a number can't be confirmed, keep "few weeks" and let the sub carry the specificity. Either way, test it against the callout version, which self-selects harder for cold traffic:

> Just had a facelift, tummy tuck or lipo? The next three weeks decide how it turns out.

## 2. Opening line and sub

> Swelling and bruising clear on oxygen's schedule. Hyperbaric oxygen gives the tissue your surgeon worked on more of what it repairs with, so you can look like yourself again sooner.

The first sentence is the best line on the page. Short, unexpected, true, and it opens a small loop (oxygen's schedule? what's that?). The second sentence closes the loop with an abstraction: "more of what it repairs with" makes the reader translate. Say the word.

**Rewrite:**

> Swelling and bruising clear on oxygen's schedule. Under pressure, your blood carries far more of it to the tissue your surgeon worked on, so you look like yourself again sooner.

## 3. Pain is never quantified or felt

The page names the problem (swelling, bruising, hiding) but never makes the reader feel a day of it. Direct response does the math or paints the scene. Compliance rules out the math (no promised timelines), so paint the scene. Two sentences, placed directly after "This is for you if," before the chamber photo:

> Week two. The swelling has moved somewhere new, the bruising has turned yellow, and there's a dinner on Saturday you already said yes to. You can wait it out. Or you can give your body more to work with while you wait.

That last line is also the bridge into the mechanism section, which the page currently lacks.

## 4. The So-What chain on the four benefits

| Card | Current depth | So what, one level deeper |
|---|---|---|
| Presentable sooner | Emotional, good ("hiding from the camera") | Keep. |
| Protect the investment | Functional ("gets more of the one thing it repairs with") | "The result you pictured when you booked the surgery is the one that shows up." |
| Nobody has to know | Emotional, good | Keep, but it duplicates the FAQ answer word for word. Vary one of them. |
| Your surgeon stays in charge | Reassurance | Fine as is. This card's job is to lower anxiety, not to sell. |

**Rewrite for card two:**

> The weeks after surgery are when your outcome is decided. Give the tissue your surgeon shaped more oxygen now, and the result you pictured when you booked is the one that shows up.

## 5. Rhythm

Three places where the rhythm flattens:

- **Benefit cards.** Four cards, four paragraphs of near-identical length and cadence. The trims for layout made this worse. Let one card be a single short sentence. "Nobody has to know" can be: "A private consultation. Sessions you can read or nap through. Nothing added to the downtime you already planned." Three fragments read faster than one sentence.
- **FAQ answers.** Five of seven open with a subordinate clause or a restatement of the question ("Decided with Dr. Haas at...", "That's exactly what...", "That's what your consultation answers"). Open two of them with a one-word answer: "Yes." / "No." / "Usually two to four weeks." then explain.
- **The expectation paragraph.** "Hyperbaric oxygen compounds. Session one is how it feels. Two to three weeks in is where recovery shows." "Compounds" is finance vocabulary and "two to three weeks in is where" is a mouthful. Rewrite:

> One session is what it feels like. Three weeks of sessions is where you see it. That's why the Kickstart is step one of a short course timed to your recovery window, not a one-off.

## 6. Specificity gaps

- **Proof is process-specific, not outcome-specific.** Kat's review is the only outcome on the page and it isn't cosmetic. The two cosmetic reviews the clinic can release should be structured as mini case studies: what they had done, how far out they were when they started, what they noticed and when, first name and city. Template to send the clinic: "I had [procedure] on [month]. I started at VYVE [n] days later. By [week], [what they noticed]. [One sentence of feeling.]" Experience, never diagnosis.
- **"Hospital wound and graft care for decades"** is the page's one authority claim and it's vague on purpose. If Dr. Haas will sign off on a tighter version ("used in hospitals for compromised grafts and slow-healing wounds"), it lands harder. If not, keep it as is.
- **"About 5 first-visit slots a week"** is exactly the kind of number that makes a page believable. Use it once more, in the hero micro copy, not only in the final CTA.

## 7. Open loops

The page has none. Three seeds, each one line, close within the next section:

- End of the benefit section, before the chamber photo: "Here's why it works when rest alone doesn't." (Closes in the mechanism section.)
- End of the mechanism section, before the mid-page CTA: "Which is exactly why we don't sell it by the session." (Closes in the Dr. Haas section.)
- End of the Dr. Haas section, before the offer: "So here's what $147 actually buys." (Closes in the offer card.)

Three is the ceiling. More than that and the device shows.

## 8. CTAs and friction reducers

**Button copy:** "Book my $147 trial session" is right. First person, names the action, price visible, no jargon. Keep it on every instance.

**Naming is split.** The button says "trial session." The offer eyebrow, the table column and the expectation paragraph say "Recovery Kickstart." A reader meets both names and has to reconcile them. Say both once, in the offer heading, then let each context use the natural one:

> The Recovery Kickstart. Your $147 trial session.

**Hero micro copy** carries the risk reversal but not the other two parts of the pattern (social proof, speed). Rewrite:

> Two minutes to book. Includes Dr. Haas's consultation, screening and your first 60-minute session. Not worth it? Say so at the desk and it's refunded on the spot.

Social proof already sits in the trust strip directly below, so the micro doesn't need to repeat it.

## 9. Disqualification

Good, and the strongest section for trust. "You can do two to three sessions a week for two to four weeks, the window that matters" is the velvet rope done right. One nit: "Your surgeon has told you not to. We'll ask, and we'll agree with them" reads as two sentences jammed together. "If your surgeon has said no, so will we." says the same in seven words.

## 10. AI tells and repetition

- **"Straight" appears four times** as a virtue word: "Straight answers" (card), "A straight answer either way" (offer), "Questions, answered straight" (FAQ eyebrow), plus "say so plainly." One tic repeated reads as a template. Keep it in the offer line and the FAQ eyebrow; rename the card "If it's not for you, we'll say so."
- **"Timed to your recovery / procedure" appears five times.** Twice is a theme, five is padding. Keep it in the offer card and the expectation paragraph; cut it from the steps and the Dr. Haas paragraph.
- **"Nothing added to the downtime you already planned"** appears verbatim in a benefit card and a FAQ answer. Vary the FAQ: "and you go home the same day, same as any afternoon."
- **Bold on key terms** in the Dr. Haas paragraph (three bolds in three sentences) is a structural tell. Bold one.
- No banned vocabulary found (no "unlock," "leverage," "journey," "elevate").

## 11. The missing founder voice

The Dr. Haas block is written about him, not by him. A first-person note of three sentences would be the highest-trust element on the page, and this audience, who just chose a surgeon by reputation, is the audience most likely to read it. Draft for him to edit, not to publish as is:

> I opened VYVE because I kept seeing hyperbaric chambers sold like tanning beds. If you've just had surgery, the last thing you need is a session rental with nobody reading your chart. So here, you see me first, we build the plan around what your surgeon has already told you, and if this isn't right for you, I'll say so.
