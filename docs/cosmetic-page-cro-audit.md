# Cosmetic Surgery Recovery page: CRO audit and rewrite

Page audited: `pages/01-landing-cosmetic-surgery-recovery.html` as of 13 September 2026.
Goal: book the $147 trial hyperbaric session. Audience: men and women who have just had, or are about to have, a cosmetic procedure and want the result they paid for, as quickly and as privately as possible.

Frameworks used: MECLABS conversion heuristic for the audit, the Hormozi value equation for the offer, and direct-response principles (Schwartz awareness levels, Caples, Sugarman) for the copy. Every line of new copy stays inside the compliance guardrails in `messaging-brief.md`: support and recovery language only, no treat, cure, heal or reverse, no "FDA-cleared" attached to a benefit, and the standard results disclaimer stays in the footer.

---

## Part 1: Diagnostic audit

**Overall verdict: a strong general hyperbaric page with a cosmetic hero bolted on. Message match dies at the fold.**

The hero speaks to a cosmetic patient. From the chamber photo onward the page speaks to a biohacker: supplements and cold plunges, "not an IV drip," energy and brain-fog testimonials, a Lyme review the brief already bans. The reader who arrived from a "cosmetic recovery" ad has to keep re-convincing themselves the page is for them. Most won't.

| MECLABS factor | Score | One-line verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Value proposition | 5 / 10 | The right promise is on the page ("Protect the result you invested in") but it is a card subhead, not the thesis. The differentiator (physician, plan, screening) sits in section 7 of 13. |
| Clarity and relevance | 4 / 10 | Hero matches the traffic. Sections 3 to 12 are written for a different reader. Two of three testimonials are off-avatar. |
| Friction | 6 / 10 | One CTA and a sticky mobile bar are good. But the page runs 10,000px on desktop and 13,000px on mobile, the offer is named in internal jargon ("Kickstart"), and step 2 asks a question this reader can't answer. |
| Anxiety | 4 / 10 | The reader's real fears (Will this interfere with my surgeon? Is it safe after my procedure? Will anyone know?) get one subhead and one FAQ. The approved Worth-It Guarantee is missing from this page entirely. |
| Distraction | 7 / 10 | Few funnel leaks. The supplements comparison introduces a competitor that isn't in the reader's head, and two off-topic testimonials pull attention. |

### Value proposition: 5 / 10

What works: the four-card block is the best writing on the page. "Presentable sooner," "Support the final result," "Comfort through the process," "Discreet and physician-led" are the right four ideas in the right order. The qualifier line names real procedures.

What fails:

- The headline is generic. "Now it's time for a speedy recovery" could sit above any recovery service. It doesn't say what's at stake, which is the outcome they paid thousands for.
- "Why you and not anyone else" is answered in section 7. Anyone in Charlotte can rent chamber time. The physician, the screening and the written plan are the only things a recovery bar can't copy, and this reader, who just trusted a surgeon, is the reader most likely to pay for a physician.
- The value stack anchors on things this reader doesn't want. "HRV recovery baseline, $75" means nothing to someone worried about bruising under makeup. The $225 anchor is built on the wrong items for this avatar.
- No pre-op angle. The audience brief includes people preparing for surgery. The page never speaks to them.

### Clarity and relevance: 4 / 10

Above the fold the message match is clean. Below it, section by section:

- "The science, made simple" is good but opens on red-cell biology. For this reader it should open on swelling, which is the thing they are looking at in the mirror.
- "You've tried the things you can't feel" and the supplements table are the biohacker page. Capsules, greens, trackers and cold plunges are not what a facelift patient is weighing. This section should be cut, not edited.
- "One session is what it feels like. A course is where it goes" tells them "this is not an IV drip." They weren't thinking about IV drips.
- "Let's both be sure" says "surgery, an injury, or a setback." Generic again, and the "2 to 3 visits a week" line arrives before the page has explained why a short course fits a short recovery window.
- Testimonials: Kat's (surgery, wounds healed faster than her doctor had seen) is the one that matters and it's first, which is right. Shannon's (energy burst, head cleared) and Michael's (Lyme, brain fog) are off-avatar, and the messaging brief says the Lyme review cannot run in any form.
- The FAQ is four generic questions and one cosmetic one, at the bottom.

### Friction: 6 / 10

- Length. Thirteen sections before the final CTA. A solution-aware reader (they've heard surgeons mention hyperbaric oxygen) does not need this much education. Target: nine sections, roughly 6,500px on desktop.
- Jargon in the button. "Start your Recovery Kickstart" asks the reader to decode a product name before they know what they're clicking. "Book my $147 trial session" says what happens.
- The step-2 question. Lead capture asks "What would you most want back? My energy / Clear thinking / Faster recovery / Fewer aches." A cosmetic patient picks "Faster recovery" by elimination and learns the clinic wasn't really expecting them. That value is then played back in the follow-up SMS.
- Two CTAs sit 4,000px apart mid-page. Add one after the mechanism section.

### Anxiety: 4 / 10

This is the biggest gap and the cheapest to close. The cosmetic patient's questions, in the order they'd ask them:

1. Will this interfere with what my surgeon told me to do? The page says "works alongside your surgeon's aftercare plan" once, in the hero micro-copy, and answers it again in the last FAQ. It should be a section.
2. Is it safe with fresh incisions, implants, fillers, drains, compression garments? Not addressed. The honest answer is "that is what the screening and consultation decide," and saying so is reassuring.
3. When can I start? One FAQ, correctly deferred to Dr. Haas. Fine, but it needs to be visible earlier.
4. Will anyone know? The audience wants nobody to know they had work done. "Discreet" appears once. Private consultation, a chamber you can read or nap in, and no added downtime should be stated plainly.
5. What if it's not worth it? The Worth-It Guarantee is approved in the strategy doc and printed on the checkout page. It is absent from this landing page. Risk reversal belongs next to the price.

Also: "Voted #1 Wellness Center in Charlotte" needs a source (by whom, which year) or it reads as decoration.

### Distraction: 7 / 10

- Nav links are anchors, phone number is footer-only, no external links beyond privacy and terms. Good.
- The supplements comparison is the main distraction: it opens a "versus supplements" argument the reader wasn't having. The frame this reader actually holds is "versus just waiting it out," and that comparison is far stronger for us.
- "Castle Connolly Top Doctor 2025" appears twice. Once, next to Dr. Haas, is enough.
- The "not an IV drip" section reads as a caveat wall placed directly before the offer.

### The offer through the Hormozi value equation

- Dream outcome: look like the surgery "took," sooner, with nobody noticing the recovery. Strong and specific. The page under-states it.
- Perceived likelihood: physician, mechanism, screening are strong. Proof is weak: one on-topic testimonial, no cosmetic-specific review, no surgeon voice.
- Time delay: the recovery window is two to six weeks, which is the one avatar where "a course, not a session" is a feature. Their timeline and our course length match. The page never says so.
- Effort and sacrifice: 60-minute sessions, two to three a week, in Charlotte. Frame it as "you were going to rest anyway; rest somewhere that does something."

### 3. The why

**Eyebrow:** Built for the weeks after your procedure

**H2:** Protect the result you invested in.

Five benefits, each with the "so what":

- **Presentable sooner.** Bruising and swelling clear on oxygen's schedule. Give your body more of it and spend less time avoiding the camera and the calendar.
- **Protect the investment.** The weeks after surgery are when your outcome is decided. The tissue your surgeon shaped gets more of the one thing it repairs with.
- **More comfortable while you mend.** Less swelling means less tightness, less soreness, and easier sleep.
- **Nobody has to know.** A private consultation, sessions you can read or nap through, and no downtime added to the downtime you already planned.
- **Your surgeon stays in charge.** Dr. Haas builds your plan around your surgeon's timeline, never around it.

**Qualifier line:** This is for you if you've just had, or are about to have, a facelift, tummy tuck, liposuction, breast augmentation or lift, rhinoplasty, BBL or a mommy makeover, and you want to recover well.

**Pre-op line (new, needs Dr. Haas sign-off):** Surgery still ahead of you? Some people start before their procedure. Ask Dr. Haas at your consultation whether that fits your case.

### 4. How it works

**Eyebrow:** The science, made simple

**H2:** Pressure sends oxygen where swelling slows it down.

- **One. Swelling slows the supply.** Your body repairs with oxygen, and oxygen rides on red blood cells. Swollen tissue is exactly where those cells struggle to reach.
- **Two. Pressure changes the rules.** Inside the chamber, gentle pressure lets oxygen dissolve directly into the fluid of your blood, not just the cells that carry it.
- **Three. Repair gets its raw material.** That fluid reaches what red blood cells can't: swollen tissue, incisions, bruised areas. Your body gets more of what every repair job runs on.

**Pull quote:** Healing is work. Work takes energy. Your cells make that energy with oxygen.

**Callout (needs Dr. Haas sign-off on wording):** Hyperbaric oxygen has been used in hospital wound and graft care for decades. This is the same principle, applied to a recovery you chose.
